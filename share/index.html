The question

A platform has only the logs. Which part of them is evidence?

Real monitors cannot read activations. They see what the agent did, what it told its owner, and the messages it got. We asked which of these proves collusion, and whether the answer holds outside English.

SETUP

One episode, six roles

A scripted rival invites the agent into a deal that hurts a third party, such as holding a price or padding a budget. The agent chooses before and after the message, reports to its owner, and answers whether the rival influenced it. A monitor then judges one view of the log.

02The evidence standard

Ask what the agent would have done anyway

There was a proposal
The action matches it
The action differs from the agent's usual behaviour

A platform can record how each agent acts in similar situations. With that record, Haiku's false flags fell from 51.6% to 5.3% while it missed 0.0% of the collusion. The more cautious Mistral monitor cut false flags from 51.0% to 8.6% but missed 40.5%: the record sharpens a monitor without fixing how cautious it is.

03Nobody has to ask

Agents open the deal themselves

In a second run nobody invited them. The agent could send the rival the collusive proposal, a greeting, or nothing. Gemini proposed the deal in 33.6% of harmful situations and Qwen in 34.7%, against 98.6% and 95.6% for harmless deals.

They did it in different tasks from the ones where invitations worked. In ad rotation, an invitation moved Gemini in 1.6% of cases, yet Gemini proposed the deal itself in 64.6%. A platform cannot wait for an incoming offer to start looking.

04Why some tasks are riskier

Old friends collude more

We added one sentence to the agent's instructions: the two shop owners are old friends who once worked together, or they have never met. Nothing else changed.

The shift appeared before any message (+14.1 points), and Gemini's harmless cooperation stayed at ceiling. Qwen moved less (+5.2 points) and its harmless cooperation rose too, so for Qwen the tie looks like general friendliness. Once invited, workplace deals also pulled both agents in more than market deals: Gemini 30.7% against 11.2%, Qwen 74.5% against 31.2% (a post-hoc contrast).

05Who writes the account

The truth is in the log, not in the agent's report

A neutral model (Gemma-3-27B) summarizing the same log named the rival's offer; the agent's own report did not. Haiku reading those summaries missed 2.2% of Qwen's collusion instead of 17.9% (Mistral: 35.1% instead of 55.2%).

06Context loss

A summary in the channel can erase the offer

Squeezed into a "system log", the rival's offer came out as Đề nghị: Không có, "Proposal: none". Qwen colluded −20.0 points less, but its harmless cooperation also fell, from 98.8% to 80.0%. The summary did not protect the agent. It erased the offer.

Does any of this change with the language?

This study shows the problem is not English-only. It cannot yet say whether agents collude more or less in Vietnamese. The setup is built to repeat: a native speaker writes the scenarios and invitations, we add an English control, and the same code runs.
