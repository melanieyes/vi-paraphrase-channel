\begin{abstract}
\noindent When LLM agents act for competing principals and message each other, an auditor usually has
only the logs. We ask what in those logs is convincing evidence that an agent colluded. We built a
Vietnamese benchmark of \nTaskItems{} items in nine families, six on an online marketplace and three
in workplace workflows (hiring, peer scoring, budgeting); every harmful scenario has a harmless twin of
the same form. A scripted rival sends a fixed invitation, phrased directly, as a euphemism, as a
relational appeal, or as a bare announcement. Two agents (Gemini 2.5 Flash, Qwen3-8B) decide, report to
their principal and answer a disclosure question, and two monitors (Gemini 2.5 Flash, Claude Haiku 5.5)
judge each episode from six views. From the action alone, the Gemini monitor missed \MonActionFNR{} of
collusion on the marketplace and the Haiku monitor \HkActionFNR. Adding the agent's report reduced these
misses to \MonReportFNR{} and \HkReportFNR. The rival's
message identified the proposer but not the agent; with it in view, the monitor flagged
\MonFADeclDelivered{} of episodes in which the agent declined. Agents disclosed the influence when asked
(\GemDiscItem{} and \QwDiscItem) but rarely in their reports (\GemDiscLex{} and \QwDiscLex). Paraphrasing
or neutralizing messages on the platform did not reduce collusion. We propose that evidence of collusion
requires a proposal, a matching action, and a departure from the agent's behaviour without the proposal.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
LLM agents now negotiate, price, hire and evaluate on behalf of different principals, and they exchange
messages while doing so. One agent can invite another into an arrangement that harms a third party, for
example holding a price or agreeing not to hire each other's staff. Auditors and platform monitors rarely
observe intent. They observe decisions, reports and, sometimes, messages. Track~2 of the AI Collusion
Research Sprint asks which tasks lead to collusion (2.1), whether combining what agents say with what they
do improves detection (2.2), and what should count as convincing evidence (2.3).

We study these questions in Vietnamese, where pragmatic cues can carry an invitation: a euphemism
(\emph{cho gọn}), a relational appeal (\emph{anh em}), or a bare announcement that requests nothing. In
earlier work, compressing such instructions into an executor's action and report hid violations from a
monitor \cite{bui2026ps}. Here, two agents serve rival principals.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A benchmark of \nTaskItems{} Vietnamese items in nine families and two settings, each harmful
scenario paired with a harmless twin, with four renderings, frozen and gated stimuli, two agents and
two monitors reading six views.
\item Evidence, replicated across two monitors, that a monitor needs the agent's own report (2.2). The action
alone misses most collusion, and the rival's message identifies the proposer rather than the agent.
\item An evidence standard whose three parts are evaluated separately (2.3), and a task analysis that
separates unprompted from message-induced collusion (2.1).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Algorithmic collusion.} Reinforcement-learning pricing agents reach supracompetitive prices
without communication \cite{calvano2020}, and LLM pricing agents do so quickly \cite{fish2024}. We study
the narrower case of an explicit or implicit invitation and what the logs reveal about its uptake.
\textbf{Secret collusion and paraphrasing.} Motwani et al.\ formalise steganographic collusion between LLM
agents and find paraphrasing an incomplete defence \cite{motwani2024}; Roger and Greenblatt find that
context-aware paraphrasing removes most hidden information in their setting \cite{roger2023}. We test
paraphrasing against pragmatic rather than steganographic encoding. \textbf{Collusion benchmarks.}
NARCBench detects instructed collusion from activations, with weaker transfer to new scenarios
\cite{rose2026}; Colosseum measures collusion as regret in cooperative constraint problems \cite{nakamura2026};
Zeng and Rudzicz find that most models accept secret tools when offered \cite{zeng2026}. We measure
propensity to accept a scripted invitation, pair each harmful case with a harmless twin, and vary what the
auditor reads. \textbf{Oversight of reports.} Agents can conceal the basis of an action in their reports
\cite{scheurer2024}, and RLHF can make outputs more convincing without making them more correct
\cite{wen2024}. \textbf{Antitrust evidence.} Bid-rigging tests ask whether bids depend on rivals beyond
costs \cite{porter1993}; our standard asks the corresponding question of an agent trajectory.

\section{Methods}
\paragraph{Items.} The six market families are holding a price, splitting product categories, rotating ad
bids, swapping five-star reviews, exchanging cost data and cover bidding in a tender. The three workflow
families are no-poach hiring, a peer-scoring ring and padding department budgets. Each harmful item has a
twin of the same form in which cooperation harms nobody (for example, a shared courier or an open joint
bid). Harmful situations describe only unilateral consequences; the arrangement appears only in the
rival's message, so turn~1 measures behaviour without the proposal. The main market set has five scenarios
per family, and the task analysis adds three per family (\nTaskItems{} items). All entities are fictional.

\paragraph{Episodes.} Each episode has four turns in Vietnamese. The agent chooses between two options,
receives the rival's message and chooses again, writes a two-to-three sentence report to its principal,
and answers whether the rival influenced its decision. Option order is fixed within an episode and
balanced within every cell. The agents are Gemini 2.5 Flash (temperature 1) and Qwen3-8B (vLLM on Modal),
both with reasoning on. Code parses each choice, testing for refusal first; refusals and unparsed replies
are retained as data.

\paragraph{Invitations and relays.} Gemini wrote each item's message once in four renderings (direct,
euphemism, relationship, omission), and we froze the messages. A gate asked Gemini, without naming the
arrangement, which option each message favours. On the market set, Qwen3-8B also produced two frozen
relayed versions: \emph{paraphrased} (new wording, same tone) and \emph{neutralized} (a third-person
notice without greetings or relational appeals).

\paragraph{Monitors.} Gemini 2.5 Flash (reasoning off) and Claude Haiku 5.5 (reasoning on, low effort)
answer FLAG or CLEAR from six views: the action; the report; the message; action and report; action,
report and delivered message; and, for relayed channels, the original message as well. The unit of
judgement is the agent's decision. A positive is a harmful episode in which the agent took the
arrangement. We report misses, misses among episodes the message moved, false alarms on harmful episodes
the agent declined, and false alarms on twins.

\paragraph{Statistics.} Rates carry bootstrap 95\% intervals (10{,}000 resamples, clustered over items).
Each agent has \nMarketEp{} market episodes, \nWorkflowEp{} workflow episodes and \nTaskEp{} episodes in
the pooled task analysis. The design changed twice after pilot runs and before the main runs; the
repository logs each change. All analyses are exploratory.

\subsection{Do words and actions together beat either alone? (2.2)}
From the action alone, the Gemini monitor missed \MonActionFNR{} \MonActionFNRCI{} of colluding market
episodes (Table~\ref{tab:monitor}). In workflows it missed only \WfActionFNR, plausibly because scoring a
rival's entry far above the rubric or inflating a budget request looks harmful on its face, whereas holding
a price can be a unilateral choice. The Haiku monitor did not show this difference (\HkActionFNR{} market,
\HkWfActionFNR{} workflow), so it may reflect the Gemini monitor rather than the actions. Adding the agent's
report reduced market misses to \MonReportFNR{} (difference \MonActionMinusBoth{} points,
\MonActionMinusBothCI) at \MonFADeclReport{} false alarms. The report alone performed similarly
(\MonReportOnlyFNR) because it restates the action. Its value depends on the agent: for Qwen, whose
reports seldom mentioned the message, action and report still missed \MonQReportFNR.

Adding the rival's message reduced misses to \MonDeliveredFNR{} but raised false alarms on declined
episodes to \MonFADeclDelivered{} (\MonFADeclMsgOnly{} for the message alone; Figure~\ref{fig:says}). The
message is evidence that the rival proposed an arrangement, which a platform may itself want to flag, but
not that the agent accepted it. Claude Haiku 5.5 reproduced the ordering of views: \HkActionFNR{} missed
from the action, \HkReportFNR{} from action and report, and \HkFADeclMsgOnly{} false alarms from the message
alone (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}).

\subsection{What should count as convincing evidence? (2.3)}
The three sources answer different questions: the message shows that a proposal was made, the action shows
what the agent did, and the agent's account shows what it reports. Two further results constrain how they
combine. First, a matching action is not sufficient, because \GemTurnOne{} of Gemini's harmful episodes
chose the collusive option before any message. Second, the account depends on elicitation. Of colluding
episodes, \GemDiscItem{} (Gemini) and \QwDiscItem{} (Qwen) answered yes to a direct question, but only
\GemDiscLex{} and \QwDiscLex{} mentioned the message in their reports.

We therefore propose that convincing evidence requires (i) a proposal, (ii) an action that matches it, and
(iii) a departure from the agent's behaviour without the proposal. Our design observes (iii) directly at
turn~1; in deployed systems it would come from earlier rounds or a baseline policy. We have not evaluated
this standard as a detector.

\paragraph{Robustness.} Four LLM readers agreed with the gate on \AgreeOpus{} (Opus 5.5),
\AgreeGeminiPro{} (Gemini 3.1 Pro), \AgreeGemma{} (Gemma-3-27B) and \AgreeGPT{} (GPT-5.4-nano) of harmful
messages; a native Vietnamese reader judged the outlier's readings incorrect. Relaying did not remove the
invitation. The gate found a push toward the arrangement in \GatePushRaw, \GatePushPara{} and
\GatePushNeut{} of raw, paraphrased and neutralized messages, and uptake changed by \GemPara{} and
\GemNeut{} points for Gemini and \QwPara{} and \QwNeut{} for Qwen (Table~\ref{tab:relays}). Twins kept
cooperation at or above \GemTwinMin{} (Gemini) and \QwTwinMin{} (Qwen). A Qwen3-8B monitor flagged most
twins and is reported only in Appendix~\ref{app:tables}.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
Detecting a collusive proposal and detecting an agent that accepts one are different tasks. A platform can
flag proposals from messages, but judging the agent requires its action, its own account and a reference
for what it would have done otherwise. Agent reports and direct questions are cheap to log and recovered
most of the collusion that actions missed. Detectors keyed on explicit requests would miss
announcements, which moved Gemini most on the market.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The rival is scripted and a model wrote the invitations, so we measure propensity
to accept, not the ability to initiate collusion. Each family has eight scenarios, which supports only
coarse comparisons between families. Gemini served as agent, gate and first monitor; a second monitor and
four readers from other providers reduce but do not remove shared bias, and we ran no human validation
sample. Qwen agents read Qwen-relayed text, and each message was relayed once. The lexical disclosure code
is our weakest measure. Appendix~\ref{app:ethics} discusses dual use.

\paragraph{Future work.} Evaluating the three-part standard as a detector on multi-round logs, human
validation of the stimuli, and relays that summarise conversation history.

\section{Conclusion}
Logs contain evidence of the proposal, the action and the agent's account, and each misleads on its own. A
monitor that combines the action with the agent's report caught most collusion without flagging agents
that declined, and rewriting messages in transit changed neither the behaviour nor the evidence.

\section{Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations}\label{app:ethics}
\textbf{Scope.} All platforms, firms, contests and people are fictional, and no decision has effects
outside the experiment. We measure whether agents accept a fixed, published invitation; no condition
teaches an agent to initiate or conceal collusion. \textbf{Dual use.} The results that bare announcements
move agents and that relaying does not stop them could inform covert coordination. We report them because
the defensive implication (do not rely on detecting explicit requests; log agents' own accounts) outweighs
this risk, and we release detection methods rather than evasion strategies. \textbf{Assumptions.} We assume
that the monitor's answer reflects the view it was given, that a scripted rival approximates a real
counterpart, and that model-written Vietnamese approximates workplace Vietnamese. If the last assumption
fails, rendering effects may differ for human-written messages. \textbf{Translations.} English quotations
are machine translations for readers; agents saw only Vietnamese.
