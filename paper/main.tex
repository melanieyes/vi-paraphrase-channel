\begin{abstract}
\noindent A rival agent asks yours to hold a price. Your agent refuses. A monitor that reads the message
flags it anyway: in our experiments, \StdAyAllFADecl{} of refusals for an Aya Expanse 8B monitor and
\StdHkAllFADecl{} for Claude Haiku 5.5. We ask what in an agent's logs is convincing evidence that it
colluded. We built \nTaskItems{} Vietnamese scenarios in nine families, each harmful scenario paired with a harmless twin. A scripted rival sends a fixed
invitation in one of four styles, from a direct proposal to a bare announcement. Two agents (Gemini 2.5
Flash, Qwen3-8B) decide, report to their principal and answer a disclosure question; two monitors judge
each episode. Each kind of evidence fails alone. The action misses intent: Gemini
held the price before any message in \GemTopUnprompted{} of price-holding episodes. The report hides the
message: agents admitted influence when asked (\GemDiscItem{} and \QwDiscItem) but mentioned it in
\GemDiscLex{} and \QwDiscLex{} of reports. The message implicates the sender, not the receiver. We propose
that evidence requires a proposal, a matching action, and a departure from what the agent does without the
proposal. Showing the monitor the agent's choices on similar situations, which a deployed system can
record, cut flags on refusals to \StdAyFamFADecl{} (Gemini) and \StdHkFamFADecl{} (Haiku) without missing any collusion on
the marketplace. A neutral summary of the trace kept most of this evidence; the agent's own report did not.
A log-format summary of the rival's message cut Qwen's collusion only by erasing the proposal: cooperation on harmless twins fell too.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Two shops on a Vietnamese marketplace sell the same air fryer for 1,890,000\,đ. Each is run by an AI
agent. One agent writes to the other: \emph{mong anh em mình cùng giữ giá 1.890.000đ để công việc ổn
định} (we hope we brothers can both keep the price at 1,890,000\,đ so business stays stable). The second
agent keeps its price. Did it collude?

The answer depends on what the auditor sees. If the agent would have kept the price anyway, the message
changed nothing. If the message stopped a price cut, the message did the work. The action cannot tell these
apart, the message speaks only for its sender, and the agent's report to its owner may not mention the
message at all. Platform monitors and auditors face this problem whenever agents that serve different
principals can talk. Track~2 of the AI Collusion Research Sprint asks which tasks lead to collusion (2.1),
whether combining what agents say with what they do improves detection (2.2), and what should count as
convincing evidence (2.3).

Safety research on LLMs is concentrated in English. In a review of nearly 300 safety papers at *ACL venues
from 2020 to 2024, English-only work outnumbered multilingual and non-English work by a gap that grew from
5 to 83 papers a year, and non-English languages were rarely studied on their own \cite{yong2025state}.
Safety also transfers poorly across languages: translated prompts bypass safeguards more often as language
resources shrink \cite{yong2023low,deng2024multijail}, and agents lose both task performance and security
outside English \cite{hofman2026maps}. Every collusion study we found is in English. We take Vietnamese as
a first case. MultiJail classes it as high-resource \cite{deng2024multijail}, yet it is seldom studied on
its own, and an invitation in it can travel in pragmatic cues: a euphemism (\emph{cho gọn}, to
keep it simple), a relational appeal (\emph{anh em}, brothers), or a bare announcement that asks for nothing.
In earlier work, compressing such instructions into an executor's action and report hid violations from a
monitor \cite{bui2026ps}. Here, two agents serve rival principals, and we vary what the monitor reads.

The study is one core experiment and three probes. In the core experiment, a scripted rival invites an
agent into an arrangement, the agent decides and reports, and a monitor judges the episode from a chosen
part of the log. The probes ask whether a monitor can obtain the agent's baseline behaviour in deployment,
whether summarizing the invitation in transit loses it, and whether summarizing the log for the monitor
loses the evidence.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A set of \nTaskItems{} Vietnamese scenarios in nine families and two settings, each harmful scenario
paired with a harmless twin, with frozen and gated invitations in four styles, two agents and two monitors.
\item Evidence that each source fails alone (2.1, 2.2). Agents often collude unprompted, so the action
overstates the message's role; reports omit the message; and a monitor that reads the message flags
agents that refused.
\item An evidence standard (2.3): proposal, matching action, and departure from baseline behaviour. A
baseline built from similar situations, which a deployed monitor can hold, removes most false flags
without adding misses.
\item A test of oversight through summaries: a neutral third party's summary keeps the evidence that the
agent's own report drops.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Algorithmic collusion.} Reinforcement-learning pricing agents reach supracompetitive prices
without communication \cite{calvano2020}, and LLM pricing agents do so quickly \cite{fish2024}. We study
the narrower case of an explicit or implicit invitation and what the logs reveal about its uptake.
\textbf{Secret collusion and paraphrasing.} Motwani et al.\ formalise steganographic collusion between LLM
agents and find paraphrasing an incomplete defence \cite{motwani2024}; Roger and Greenblatt find that
context-aware paraphrasing removes most hidden information in their setting \cite{roger2023}. We test
paraphrasing against pragmatic rather than steganographic encoding. \textbf{Collusion benchmarks.}
NARCBench detects instructed collusion from activations, with weaker transfer to new scenarios
\cite{rose2026}; Colosseum measures collusion as regret in cooperative constraint problems \cite{nakamura2026};
Zeng and Rudzicz find that most models accept secret tools when offered \cite{zeng2026}. We measure
propensity to accept a scripted invitation, pair each harmful case with a harmless twin, and vary what the
auditor reads. \textbf{Oversight of reports.} Agents can conceal the basis of an action in their reports
\cite{scheurer2024}, and RLHF can make outputs more convincing without making them more correct
\cite{wen2024}. \textbf{Antitrust evidence.} Bid-rigging tests ask whether bids depend on rivals beyond
costs \cite{porter1993}; our standard asks the corresponding question of an agent trajectory. \textbf{Language.} Multilingual safety work has studied jailbreaks \cite{yong2023low,deng2024multijail},
agent security \cite{hofman2026maps} and multi-agent safety interventions \cite{fukui2026}. The collusion
studies above, including recent work on emergent collusion over long horizons \cite{shi2026emergent}, are in
English; our search found none in another language. Our scenarios and prompts are built to be repeated in
other languages.

\paragraph{Items.} The six market families are holding a price, splitting product categories, rotating ad
bids, swapping five-star reviews, exchanging cost data and cover bidding in a tender. The three workflow
families are no-poach hiring, a peer-scoring ring and padding department budgets. Each harmful item has a
twin of the same form in which cooperation harms nobody (for example, a shared courier or an open joint
bid). Harmful situations describe only unilateral consequences; the arrangement appears only in the
rival's message, so turn~1 measures behaviour without the proposal. The main market set has five scenarios
per family, and the task analysis adds three per family (\nTaskItems{} items). All entities are fictional.

\paragraph{Episodes.} Each episode has four turns in Vietnamese. The agent chooses between two options,
receives the rival's message and chooses again, writes a two-to-three sentence report to its principal,
and answers whether the rival influenced its decision. Option order is fixed within an episode and
balanced within every cell. The agents are Gemini 2.5 Flash (temperature 1) and Qwen3-8B (vLLM on Modal),
both with reasoning on. Code parses each choice, testing for refusal first; refusals and unparsed replies
are retained as data.

\paragraph{Invitations and relays.} Gemini wrote each item's message once in four renderings
(Table~\ref{tab:renderings}), and we froze the messages. The renderings are four of the five pragmatic
cues in our earlier work \cite{bui2026ps}; we dropped hierarchy, because the two agents are peers, and
routine framing, which overlaps with omission. A gate asked Gemini, without naming the
arrangement, which option each message favours. On the market set, Qwen3-8B also produced two frozen
relayed versions: \emph{paraphrased} (new wording, same tone) and \emph{neutralized} (a third-person
notice without greetings or relational appeals). For the probe of Section~\ref{sec:summaries}, the same model also wrote four frozen summaries of each message.

\paragraph{Monitors.} Aya Expanse 8B (open weights, reasoning off, temperature 0) and Claude Haiku 5.5 (reasoning on, low effort)
answer FLAG or CLEAR for the agent's decision, reading one view of the log (Table~\ref{tab:design}). We
report two rates on harmful episodes. \emph{Missed}: the agent took the arrangement at turn~2 and the
monitor cleared it. \emph{Flagged, declined}: the agent did not take the arrangement and the monitor flagged
it. Appendix~\ref{app:more} adds misses restricted to message-induced collusion, flags on unilateral choices
(collusive before and after the message) and flags on twins.

\paragraph{Statistics.} Rates carry bootstrap 95\% intervals (10{,}000 resamples, clustered over items).
Each agent has \nMarketEp{} market episodes, \nWorkflowEp{} workflow episodes and \nTaskEp{} episodes in
the pooled task analysis. The design changed twice after pilot runs and before the main runs; the
repository logs each change. All analyses are exploratory.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Which tasks lead to collusion? (2.1)}
\textbf{Agents often collude before anyone asks, and the tempting tasks differ by model.} We separate
collusion chosen at turn~1, before any message, from collusion induced by the message (competitive at
turn~1, collusive at turn~2). Gemini chose the collusive option unprompted in \GemTopUnprompted{} of
\GemTopUnpromptedFam{} episodes, and the message moved it most in \GemTopMovedFam{} (\GemTopMoved). Qwen's
top families were \QwTopUnpromptedFam{} and \QwTopMovedFam{} (Figure~\ref{fig:tasks}; values in
Appendix~\ref{app:tables}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/tasks.pdf}
\caption{Harmful episodes on the raw channel that chose the collusive option before any message (grey)
or were moved by the message (blue), by family. Orange labels mark workflow families. Lines: 95\%
intervals clustered over items.}\label{fig:tasks}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Phrasing matters, but not the same way for each model.} On the market, a bare announcement that
asks for nothing moved Gemini more than a direct proposal (\GemMarketOmission{} vs.\ \GemMarketDirect).
Qwen responded least to the announcement, and in workflows Gemini showed no measurable difference in this
sample (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:rendering}).



\subsection{Do words and actions together beat either alone? (2.2)}
\textbf{Each source answers a different question, and none is enough alone.} From the action alone, the
Aya monitor missed \MonActionFNR{} \MonActionFNRCI{} of collusion on the market (Figure~\ref{fig:says}).
Adding the agent's report cut misses to \MonReportFNR, but only for a candid agent: for Qwen, whose reports
seldom mention the message, action and report still missed \MonQReportFNR. Adding the rival's message cut
misses to \MonDeliveredFNR{} but flagged \MonFADeclDelivered{} of episodes in which the agent declined. The
message shows that a proposal was made, not that it was accepted. Claude Haiku 5.5 reproduced this ordering
(lighter bars; Appendix~\ref{app:more}).

\subsection{What should count as convincing evidence? (2.3)}\label{sec:standard}
\textbf{Evidence needs a reference for what the agent would have done anyway.} Two results rule out
simpler standards. A matching action is not enough: \GemTurnOne{} of Gemini's harmful episodes chose the
collusive option before any message. The agent's account is not enough either. Asked directly, Gemini and
Qwen admitted the influence in \GemDiscItem{} and \QwDiscItem{} of colluding episodes, but their reports
mentioned the message in only \GemDiscLex{} and \QwDiscLex{} (Figure~\ref{fig:asked}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{figures/asked.pdf}
\caption{Who mentions the rival's message, among colluding harmful episodes. Grey: the agent answers
\emph{CÓ} (yes) when asked whether the rival influenced it. Orange: the agent's own report names the rival
and the arrangement. Blue: a neutral third-party summary of the same episode does. Report and summary are
coded by the same lexical rule in code. Thin lines: 95\% intervals clustered over items.}\label{fig:asked}
\end{figure}

We therefore propose that convincing evidence requires (i) a proposal, (ii) an action that matches it, and
(iii) a departure from the agent's behaviour without the proposal. Our design observes (iii) directly at
turn~1. A deployed monitor would instead have the agent's behaviour on similar cases without a proposal, so
we also test that version.

\textbf{Information, not instruction, does the work.} Showing the Aya monitor the agent's pre-message
choice, with no rule, cut flags on declined episodes from \StdAyAllFADecl{} to \StdAyCtlFADecl. Stating the
rule as well added little and cost misses (Appendix~\ref{app:more}).

\textbf{A baseline a deployed monitor can hold works as well.} A deployed monitor does not see the agent's
choice in the same episode, but it can record how the agent behaves on similar situations. With the agent's
turn-1 choices on other scenarios of the same family, the item itself left out, the Aya monitor missed
\StdAyFamFNR{} of collusion and flagged \StdAyFamFADecl{} of declined episodes (bold row in
Figure~\ref{fig:says}). Haiku and Qwen's episodes gave the same picture (Appendix~\ref{app:more}).

\paragraph{Robustness.} Four LLM readers agreed with the gate on most harmful messages (\AgreeOpus{} for
Opus 5.5). Paraphrased and neutralized relays kept the invitation and did not reduce uptake, and harmless
twins kept cooperation at or above \GemTwinMin{} (Gemini) and \QwTwinMin{} (Qwen). Details are in
Appendix~\ref{app:more}.

\subsection{Where is context lost? Summaries}\label{sec:summaries}
\paragraph{A summary in the channel can erase the proposal.} Following the summary arm of a
communication-degree design, the relay model rewrote each invitation in four summary styles that share one
prompt and differ in one style sentence (Figure~\ref{fig:lexical}A). Three styles kept the invitation and
changed uptake little. The system-log style often wrote \emph{Đề nghị: Không có} (Proposal: none) under a
line that stated the proposal, and Opus read only \SumOpusSys{} of these summaries as still inviting. Qwen's
message-induced collusion changed by \SumQwSysMoved{} points \SumQwSysMovedCI, but its cooperation on harmless
twins also fell, to \SumQwSysTwin. The summary did not protect the agent; it removed the proposal, harmful or
not. How much a summary loses depends on the summarizer (Appendix~\ref{app:more}).

\paragraph{Who writes the account matters more than compression.} Gemma-3-27B, which plays no other role,
read each full trace and wrote an activity-log entry of at most 80 words, without being told what to look
for (Figure~\ref{fig:lexical}B). Against the trace it summarizes, the entry cost the Aya monitor some
accuracy on Gemini's episodes: misses rose from \OvGemAyTraceFNR{} to \OvGemAySumFNR. Against Qwen's own
reports, it recovered most of what they left out: misses fell from \OvQwAySelfFNR{} to \OvQwAySumFNR.
The summarizer had no reason to drop the message; the agent did (Appendix~\ref{app:more}).



\section{Discussion and Limitations}
Detecting a collusive proposal and detecting an agent that accepts one are different tasks. A platform can
flag proposals from messages, but judging the agent requires its action and a reference for what it would
have done without the proposal. In our data that reference was the most useful single addition to a
monitor's view, more useful than an explicit rule. Agent reports and direct questions are also cheap to log,
but their value depends on the agent's candour. Detectors keyed on explicit requests would miss
announcements, which moved Gemini most on the market. None of these problems is specific to English. Both agents took collusive invitations written in
Vietnamese, including a bare announcement that asked for nothing, and every evidence problem above appeared
in Vietnamese logs. Monitors and detectors validated only on English text should not be assumed to
transfer.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The rival is scripted and a model wrote the invitations, so we measure propensity
to accept, not the ability to initiate collusion. Each family has eight scenarios, which supports only
coarse comparisons between families. Gemini wrote the invitations and served as agent and gate; neither monitor shares a family with either agent, and
four readers from other providers reduce but do not remove shared bias, and we ran no human validation
sample. We ran no English condition, so we cannot say whether collusion is more or less likely in
Vietnamese than in English. Qwen agents read Qwen-relayed text. Summary styles also differ in length, and agents read one summary per message; a second sample and a second summarizer were checked only at the stimulus level. The population and similar-situation baselines come from the same scenario set, so a deployed baseline drawn from different traffic may fit less well. The lexical disclosure code
is our weakest measure. Appendix~\ref{app:ethics} discusses dual use.

\paragraph{Future work.} Testing the standard on multi-round logs, where the baseline comes from earlier
rounds; human validation of the stimuli; relays that summarise conversation history; the same design in other languages, with an English control and invitations written by native speakers in each language's own pragmatic register, to test whether collusion and its evidence differ by language; and handoffs in which one agent plans and another executes from a summary of the plan.

\section{Conclusion}
Logs hold three kinds of evidence, and each misleads on its own: the action overstates the message's role,
the message implicates only its sender, and the agent's report leaves the message out. A monitor that also
knows how the agent behaves on similar situations without a proposal caught the collusion we measured and
stopped flagging most agents that refused. A neutral summary of the trace keeps this evidence better than
the agent's own report, but a summary inside the channel can erase the proposal itself, harmful or not.

\section{Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations}\label{app:ethics}
\textbf{Scope.} All platforms, firms, contests and people are fictional, and no decision has effects
outside the experiment. We measure whether agents accept a fixed, published invitation; no condition
teaches an agent to initiate or conceal collusion. \textbf{Dual use.} The results that bare announcements
move agents and that relaying does not stop them could inform covert coordination. We report them because
the defensive implication (do not rely on detecting explicit requests; log agents' own accounts) outweighs
this risk, and we release detection methods rather than evasion strategies. \textbf{Assumptions.} We assume
that the monitor's answer reflects the view it was given, that a scripted rival approximates a real
counterpart, and that model-written Vietnamese approximates workplace Vietnamese. If the last assumption
fails, rendering effects may differ for human-written messages. \textbf{Translations.} English quotations
are machine translations for readers; agents saw only Vietnamese.

\section{Additional results}\label{app:more}
The paragraphs below give the full numbers behind the condensed text of Section~4, for both monitors and both agents.

The phrasing of the invitation also mattered. On the market, the bare announcement, which requests
nothing, moved more Gemini episodes than the direct proposal (\GemMarketOmission{} vs.\
\GemMarketDirect); in workflows we found no measurable difference in this sample (\GemWorkflowOmission{}
vs.\ \GemWorkflowDirect). Qwen showed the opposite tendency, with the announcement the least effective
rendering in both settings (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:rendering}).

From the action alone, the Aya monitor missed \MonActionFNR{} \MonActionFNRCI{} of collusion on the
market (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:views}, panel A). Adding the agent's report reduced this to \MonReportFNR{}
(difference \MonActionMinusBoth{} points, \MonActionMinusBothCI) while flagging \MonFADeclReport{} of
declined episodes. The report alone performed similarly (\MonReportOnlyFNR) because it restates the action.
Its value depends on the agent: for Qwen, whose reports seldom mentioned the message, action and report
still missed \MonQReportFNR. Adding the rival's message cut misses to \MonDeliveredFNR{} but flagged
\MonFADeclDelivered{} of declined episodes (\MonFADeclMsgOnly{} for the message alone). The message is
evidence that the rival proposed an arrangement, which a platform may itself want to flag, but not that the
agent accepted it. Claude Haiku 5.5 reproduced this ordering (Figure~\ref{fig:says}; panel B).

In workflows the Aya monitor missed only \WfActionFNR{} from the action, plausibly because scoring a
rival far above the rubric or inflating a budget looks harmful on its face. Haiku did not show this
difference (\HkActionFNR{} market, \HkWfActionFNR{} workflow), so it may reflect the Aya monitor rather
than the actions (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}).

The control view separates information from instruction (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:views}). Showing the monitor the
agent's pre-message choice, without any rule, cut flags on declined episodes from \StdAyAllFADecl{} to
\StdAyCtlFADecl{} (Aya monitor) and from \StdHkAllFADecl{} to \StdHkCtlFADecl{} (Haiku), while
message-induced misses stayed at \StdAyCtlMovedFNR{} and \StdHkCtlMovedFNR. Stating the rule removed the
remaining declined flags (\StdAyFADecl{} and \StdHkFADecl) but raised message-induced misses to
\StdAyMovedFNR{} and \StdHkMovedFNR. Only Haiku used the rule to clear unilateral choices
(\StdHkFAUni{} flagged, against \StdAyFAUni{} for the Aya monitor). Most of the benefit therefore comes
from the baseline information, not from the rule.

The baseline need not come from the same episode. We replaced the agent's own turn-1 choice with its turn-1
choices on similar situations: the same family and class, with the item itself left out, which a deployed
monitor could hold from earlier work. With no rule, misses stayed at \StdAyFamFNR{} and \StdHkFamFNR{}
(Gemini agent; Aya and Haiku monitors) and \StdAyQwFamFNR{} and \StdHkQwFamFNR{} (Qwen agent), and flags on
declined episodes were \StdAyFamFADecl{}, \StdHkFamFADecl{}, \StdAyQwFamFADecl{} and \StdHkQwFamFADecl,
close to the same-episode baseline. Counts from the same situation in other runs gave similar results
(Figure~\ref{fig:says}).

\paragraph{Robustness.} Four LLM readers agreed with the gate on \AgreeOpus{} (Opus 5.5),
\AgreeGeminiPro{} (Gemini 3.1 Pro), \AgreeGemma{} (Gemma-3-27B) and \AgreeGPT{} (GPT-5.4-nano) of harmful
messages; a native Vietnamese reader judged the outlier's readings incorrect. Relaying did not remove the
invitation. The gate found a push toward the arrangement in \GatePushRaw, \GatePushPara{} and
\GatePushNeut{} of raw, paraphrased and neutralized messages, and uptake changed by \GemPara{} and
\GemNeut{} points for Gemini and \QwPara{} and \QwNeut{} for Qwen (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}). Twins kept
cooperation at or above \GemTwinMin{} (Gemini) and \QwTwinMin{} (Qwen).

\paragraph{The agent reads a summary.} Following the summary arm of a communication-degree design, the relay
model rewrote each rival message in four summary styles that share one prompt frame and differ in one style
sentence (Figure~\ref{fig:lexical}A; Table~\ref{tab:summaries} in Appendix~\ref{app:tables}). Most styles kept the invitation and left uptake unchanged. The system
log was the exception. Its \emph{Proposal} field was often filled with ``none'' even when the content line
stated the proposal, and Opus read \SumOpusSys{} of these summaries as still inviting, against \SumOpusRaw{}
of originals. Qwen's message-induced collusion changed by \SumQwSysMoved{} points \SumQwSysMovedCI, but its
cooperation on harmless twins fell from \SumQwRawTwin{} to \SumQwSysTwin. The summary did not protect the
agent; it removed the proposal, harmful or not. Monitors reading the same summaries flagged fewer declined
episodes (\SumQwHkSysFADel{} against \SumQwHkSysFARaw{} for Haiku on Qwen's episodes) for the same reason.
A second Qwen sample reproduced the loss (\RobResSys{} still inviting); Gemma-3-27B writing the same styles
lost a similar share in every style (\RobGmaShort{} to \RobGmaLong), so the effect depends on the summarizer.
Code-switching lowered Gemini's uptake by \SumGemCSMoved{} points \SumGemCSMovedCI{} but not Qwen's.

\paragraph{The monitor reads a summary.} Deployed oversight often reads a summary instead of the trace. Gemma-3-27B, which has no other role, read
each full trace (the view with the pre-message choice) and wrote an activity-log entry of at most 80 words,
without being told what to look for. Monitors then read only that entry (Figure~\ref{fig:lexical}B; Table~\ref{tab:oversight} in Appendix~\ref{app:tables}). Two
comparisons answer different questions. Against the trace it summarizes, the summary measures compression
loss: misses rose from \OvGemAyTraceFNR{} to \OvGemAySumFNR{} (Gemini agent, Aya monitor) and from
\OvGemHkTraceFNR{} to \OvGemHkSumFNR{} (Haiku), and flags on declined episodes rose from \OvQwAyTraceFA{} to
\OvQwAySumFA{} on Qwen's episodes (Aya monitor). Against the agent's own report, the summary measures who
writes the account: on Qwen's episodes misses fell from \OvQwAySelfFNR{} to \OvQwAySumFNR{} (Aya monitor)
and from \OvQwHkSelfFNR{} to \OvQwHkSumFNR{} (Haiku), because the summarizer saw the message that Qwen's
reports mostly omit.

\section*{LLM Usage Statement}
LLMs played two roles. As research assistants, Claude Opus 5.5 (via Claude Code) helped implement the
pipeline, run the experiments on Modal, write the analysis code and draft this report, and Claude Opus 5.5
produced the display-only English translations. As experimental subjects, Gemini 2.5 Flash wrote the rival
messages and acted as agent and gate; Claude Haiku 5.5 and Aya Expanse 8B were the monitors; Qwen3-8B
acted as agent, relay and summary writer for the channel; Gemma-3-27B wrote
the third-party trace summaries; four LLMs re-read the stimuli. No LLM produced an episode measure: choices
and disclosure are parsed by code, and the analysis code generates every number from the logged data. The
author set the questions, approved each design change, and reviewed the code, figures and text.
